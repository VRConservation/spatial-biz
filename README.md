Tools used to plan conservation projects, such as Miradi and the Open Standards for the Practice of Conservation, help practitioners carry out their work systematically, but they provide little support for the geospatial analysis and mapping essential to conservation strategies. Furthermore, conservation action-planning methodologies do not include an empirical evaluation of landscape-level ecological conditions, which means practitioners must carry out geospatial analysis as a separate, independent activity. Drawing on examples from the Marin County Forest Health Strategy—such as fire history, the spread of young Douglas-fir beyond recent fire perimeters, and areas mapped as departing from desired conditions—we examine cases where geospatial analysis is directly integrated into these conservation planning frameworks. We show how situation models, theories of change, and results chains connect the variables that drive ecosystems and business operations to conservation objectives, treatments, and measurable outcomes. We then describe a learning-and-adaptation feedback loop and provide readers with the Forest Business Alliance templates and guidance for implementing these integrated practices across large landscapes.
